When he was admitted to university and began receiving a top scholarship of 200,000 won a month, a large sum, his father demanded that he hand the money over. His father said he would use it to pay the cram school fees of his older brother, who had worked on construction sites and finished the university entrance equivalency exam the hard way. But knowing well that his father poured everything into buying a house, he doubted his father would fully support his brother's studies. Just as he himself had passed the equivalency exams with money earned through blood and sweat, he believed his brother's future deserved full investment too. So he firmly pushed back, saying he would rent a small room in Seoul, live there with his brother, and pay his brother's cram school fees and living costs himself. As word spread that he had entered law school as a top scholarship student, the people around him began treating him as if he had already passed the bar and become a judge or prosecutor. He had started studying because he did not want to live as a miserable worker, beaten and bullied in a factory, yet he could not stop thinking of his young factory friends who must still be working through the night on cold factory floors. Around the age of twenty, before his university entrance ceremony, he wrote a resolution in his diary: even if he passed the bar exam, instead of becoming a judge or prosecutor he would become a lawyer, help the powerless and marginalized, and be a warm light for those suffering in the shadows.

University life was very different from what he had imagined. Unlike his factory days, he had only one or two classes a day, and more free time than he knew what to do with. But unexpected new hurdles appeared. His law and economics books were full of Chinese characters, and because he had crammed for the equivalency exams in a short time, he could not read them properly. As he struggled over a character dictionary, his father came over and fluently taught him the characters he did not know, and seeing this new side of a father who had only ever been stern, he felt an odd tenderness. Never having attended a regular high school, he could not keep up with the drills or bayonet practice in military training classes, and he was forever stumbling and becoming a laughingstock. Having learned English alone from books and dictionaries, he had never had the chance to hear how words actually sounded, and he quietly suffered humiliations such as pronouncing "Bible" as "bib-leh," "iron" as "eye-rong," and "island" as "iz-land." He went on group blind dates and to dance halls, but no one paid attention to a freshman who looked like a poor returning student in a worn military-training uniform and rubber shoes. When his plan to rent a room in Seoul collapsed in the face of his father's fierce opposition, he worked out a compromise: he would receive the scholarship first, set aside his brother's cram school fees and book money, and give the rest to the family. In a cramped one-room basement where a family of six lived and breathed on top of one another, he kept the light on until dawn, turning the pages of law books and praying earnestly for his family's peace and harmony even in poverty.

In the summer vacation of his first year, for the first time in his life, he set off on a free backpacking trip with his friend Shim Jeong-un. With the 14,000 won they had saved by sharing textbooks, they bought fishing rods, backpacks and cheap sneakers and headed for Gangwon Province. Traveling by boat on the Soyang River, they happened to meet an engineering student from their university who was cycling around the country, and he became their traveling companion. Late one evening after sunset, despite soldiers' attempts to stop them, the three walked all night over the steep Gwangchiryeong Pass, and the journey continued to Hangyeryeong Pass, Socheongbong Peak on Seoraksan and Biseondae. His cheap basketball shoes covered his feet with blisters, so he climbed the rough rocky mountain barefoot. Crossing those steep mountain paths, he learned life's natural order, that after every endless climb there is always a descent, and that the deeper the valley, the higher the peak that rises above it; and he came to understand that hope, which keeps us moving even in despair, is a power given equally to every human being.

Meeting Lee Young-jin, a classmate in the same department, became a major turning point in his life. Until then he had believed only the distorted reports of the government, newspapers and broadcasters, and had wrongly understood the May 18 Gwangju Democratization Movement as a riot by mobs. In their club room Lee Young-jin showed him video footage recording the horrific massacre that had taken place in Gwangju. Watching martial-law troops brutally suppress and kill ordinary, unarmed citizens, he was overwhelmed by shock and rage beyond words, and swept by intense shame and guilt at having been deceived by the regime's lies into misjudging innocent citizens. His friend proposed that they join the student movement together against the unjust dictatorship, but he could not throw himself into the movement, ready to go to prison at any moment, while turning his back on his family's poverty and the family members sweating day and night in factories. Instead he made a firm promise: if he later passed the bar, he would give up the path of becoming a high-ranking judge or prosecutor, become a lawyer, and devote his whole life to workers and to the weak who had been wronged.

In the spring of 1984, when he turned twenty, he passed the first round of the bar exam on his first attempt with a high score. He heard the news when he arrived in Daegu after cycling with his brother along the west and south coasts, and that same year his father achieved his lifelong goal of owning a home, so the whole family laughed together again for the first time in a long while. He immediately moved into the Gwanak exam lodging house to prepare for the second round, but on campus there were daily protests demanding democracy, thick with the smell of tear gas. While his classmates set their studies aside and fought at the risk of going to prison, he could not simply shout slogans from a comfortable distance. When the protests turned into fierce stone-throwing battles with the police, unable to overcome his guilty conscience, he joined the ranks, drenched in tear gas, and threw stones so hard that the skin of his scalp peeled. Tormented by the sense that he was in debt to friends who were throwing their whole selves into the pain of their times, he sometimes drank and climbed the hill to the lodging house, reminding himself again and again that the only way to repay that debt was to keep, without fail, his promise to become a lawyer and stand with the weak.

In July 1985 he sat the second round, carrying everyone's expectations. Confident that the exam had gone well, he spent eighteen days cycling across the country to the southern tip of the peninsula and back, but his name was not on the list of those who passed. His total score was well above the pass mark, but in commercial law he scored 39.66, just 0.34 points short of the subject's minimum of 40, and so he failed. The complacency that came from passing the first round so quickly, and the mistake of not examining the questions carefully, had led to a painful failure. Never having failed an exam before, he wept with grief and fell into such deep turmoil that he even lost his bag. Not knowing how he could afford another year, he shut himself away in his room, and then, unexpectedly, his father quietly encouraged him, telling him to go on a trip. He visited his hometown, met old friends and steadied himself, and thanks to this painful failure he let go of his arrogance and became deeper and more serious in how he approached the world. Instead of wavering between the studies in front of him and the responsibilities of his time, he took part openly in the main rallies while keeping up his studies without fail, and grew into a steady person who could balance the present and the future.
