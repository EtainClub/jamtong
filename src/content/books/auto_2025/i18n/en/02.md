On the morning he was finally to be paid three months of overdue wages, he walked four kilometers to the necklace factory in Changgok-dong, only to find its gates shut tight. The owner had pocketed the workers' wages and fled in the night. Because the thirteen-year-old had been working illegally under someone else's name, he could not even report it to the authorities. The pay for ninety days of grueling work, twelve hours a day, had vanished, and when he came home and saw his mother tearing dried pollack into strips for piecework, tears of grief and injustice poured out of him. He then found a job at Dongma Rubber, a factory making rubber parts for capacitors. His task was to grind the parts against sandpaper on a motor-driven polisher, and the skin of his palms wore away until his fingerprints were gone and his hands bled. Day after day he worked overtime until ten at night or all-night shifts until two in the morning, and because of the curfew he slept curled up on the cold factory floor, learning popular songs from other boy workers his age. Then one day his finger was caught in the polisher. He never got proper treatment, and black rubber dust stayed lodged under his fingernail for good. He did not even know there was a law that let injured workers receive part of their wages while recovering, so to get his full pay he ran the machine with one hand while his left hand was in a cast. His mother, who did the hard work of cleaning a public toilet at the market, was always anxious that her son would get hurt, and she came to wait for him outside the factory late at night when overtime ended and at four in the morning after all-night shifts. Holding her rough, warm hand as they climbed the steep hill in Sangdaewon-dong, he forgot the day's exhaustion and felt happy.

Every time he went to and from the factory in his work clothes, he envied the students his age in their school uniforms with white collars. Too ashamed to pass them, he always took back alleys. Then one day he saw a boy at the factory who wore a school uniform, and learned that he attended the night class of a civic school for working youth. Burning with a desire to learn, he went home and begged his father to send him to night school. But his father flatly refused, saying it was not a regular school. His father had once worked his way through college in Daegu before dropping out, and had been a teacher and a police officer, but after coming up to Seongnam he had been gripped by the desperate need to escape hunger and had become a man who did nothing but save money, relentlessly. The whole family had to earn, and the boy found it hard to understand a father who blocked even his young son's wish to study. The night he was refused, he pulled the blanket over his head and wept bitterly, and from then on began a long and wearing conflict with his father over finding a way to learn.

At his mother's urging, since she could not bear her son coming home with bloody palms every day, he moved to Aju Refrigeration, a factory that made refrigerators. But it was an even more dangerous and brutal workplace. He was given the shearing machine: you set a thick steel sheet on the machine and stepped on the pedal, and a huge blade came down like a guillotine to cut it. Managers in military uniforms, under the pretext of instilling discipline, made the boy workers hold push-up positions and beat them with clubs on the way in each morning, on the way out, and whenever there was a defect. One day he witnessed a horrifying accident in which an older worker beside him had his fingers cut off by the shearing machine. The sight of that worker, his face twisted into a grimace of a smile, gathering his severed fingers into a bag and running out of the factory screaming, became a nightmare he could never erase. Once you came to work in the morning you could not leave the factory even at lunchtime; it was like a prison. The only people he could lean on there were other poor boy workers in the same situation. For friends who lived on their own and went without meals, he asked his mother to pack an extra lunch box, and they shared the cold rice and a little warmth. Cut by sharp steel sheets and worn down by toxic substances and noise, his body came to bear more than a hundred scars, large and small.

When Aju Refrigeration closed, he went to Daeyang Industrial, a glove factory his father had found for him. To escape being a mere helper, he used his unusual knack for machines to learn quickly, and earlier than others his age he was put in sole charge of a heavy press. But the more factories he moved through, the more dangerous the machines became. At that time the factory managers and senior workers would gather the new boy workers in a storeroom at lunchtime, force boxing gloves onto them and make them fight. It was cruel bullying: the older men bet ice cream among themselves, and the young boy who lost had to pay for the ice cream, a sum equal to a full day's wage. Small and weak, the boy was often beaten one-sidedly and then robbed of the money he had worked so hard for. Around that time an old, heavy press came down on his left wrist in a serious accident. His wrist bone was broken, but afraid that if he showed any pain his machine would be taken away and he would be fired, he simply put ointment on the broken wrist and kept the machine running in silence. Untreated in time, the bone grew deformed and finally set, leaving a permanent physical disability he would carry for life.

In a factory full of beatings, extortion and oppression, there was one person who was never beaten and who came and went from the factory freely: Assistant Manager Hong, a supervisor said to have eyes like a frog. The reason he could lord it over the factory was simply that he had graduated from high school. Seeing this, the boy set himself three goals: to live without being beaten by others, to earn money and escape poverty, and to travel the world freely. Instead of night school, he decided to attend an evening cram school for the middle-school equivalency exam, which was a little over three months away, and won his father's permission. With no money for the fare, every day after work he ran and walked three kilometers to the cram school. He swallowed his anger at a reality in which students his age got discounted bus fares while he had to pay full fare because he was a factory boy. To fight off sleep he splashed cold water on his face and threw everything he had into his studies amid the reek of the toilets. When, for the first time in his life, he was praised for studying well, the joy of learning welled up in him. At the cram school he met Shim Jeong-un, another former factory boy, and they spurred each other on. When he tried to devote himself fully to studying a month before the exam, his father stood in the way and told him to go back to the factory, but his mother stepped in, telling her son to study and that she would pay his tuition even if she had to sell her underclothes, and so she stood up to his father and opened the way for him to learn.

Barely knowing the alphabet, with the multiple-choice strategies his teacher showed him and desperate effort, he achieved the miracle of passing every subject of the middle-school equivalency exam within three months. But the joy did not last. His father again pressed him to get a job, waking him at three in the morning to collect rubbish across the neighborhood and gather scrap. Unable to bear the shame of running into schoolgirls on their way to class in the morning, he eventually went back to the factory. Yet with a middle-school qualification in hand, nothing about factory life had changed; the beatings and forced boxing matches went on. One day an innocent boy worker put a note in the factory suggestion box asking for an end to the violence. When Assistant Manager Hong read it, he went to the foreman and the senior workers and coldly signaled his displeasure, and the moment he did, the supervisors gave the boy workers a merciless group beating. As the boys lay sprawled and covered in blood, Hong strolled in as if nothing had happened and surveyed the factory with a satisfied look. It was the moment the cruel hypocrisy of a ruler was laid bare: one who kept the system running by using his power to incite violence without ever getting blood on his own hands. Suffering a cracked rib from a beating at the factory, he came to feel keenly that the superiors who ordered the violence and built the structure to profit from it were far more vicious than those who swung the fists. And so he moved away from his original idea of becoming a manager like Assistant Manager Hong and lording it over others, toward the conviction that he had to build a world without people like Hong who exploit and oppress the powerless.
