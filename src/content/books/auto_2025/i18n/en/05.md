Once, while preparing for the bar exam in his lodging house, he heard a racket outside and irritably flung the window open. Looking out, he saw a middle-aged woman climbing up and down the precarious temporary stairs of a construction site with heavy bricks balanced on her head. The moment his eyes met those of the sweating woman, his irritation turned to shame, and a deep ache spread through his chest. Even as he sat in the lodging house poring over books, the image of his mother and siblings working themselves to the bone for twelve hours a day on construction sites and in factories rose before him. Seeing that what that woman, or his young sister working in a factory, earned for a month of blood and sweat came nowhere near the cost of his room and board, a deep question and anger rose within him as to whether such an order of the world could be just.

Another time, an older exam candidate from the next room came over and swore at him for being noisy. He meant to apologize quietly, but the moment the man sneered that they were as rowdy as factory boys, he could not stand it and hit back hard. A senior who studied with him broke up the fight, bought him a drink and tried to calm him, saying the man was on edge from exam stress and he should let it go; but he could not swallow his anger, insisting that if the man wanted to swear, he should swear at the ones who had been noisy, not drag in and insult workers who had done nothing wrong and were sweating in factories. When the senior asked why he was so sensitive to the word "factory boy" when he was already a university student and would soon pass the exam and gain social standing, he raised his voice: just because he had left that place, did that mean the pain of the countless boy workers still slaving over oil and grease in factories had disappeared? A person who starts out in a poor and marginalized place, he believed, ends up on one of two paths: erasing his past and getting drunk on the sweetness of privilege, or never forgetting his roots and devoting his life to solving that structural suffering. Since he had not come this far just to live comfortably on his own, he firmly resolved never to forget the reality of the poor and powerless people who were his own origins.

As the second semester of his senior year ended, the fates of his classmates from poor families diverged sharply. Classmates who were well off stayed in the exam lodging houses of Sillim-dong and kept studying, while friends with no money and no one to lean on had to give up their studies and were dragged off to the army one after another as soon as they graduated. Meanwhile, the children of families with money and power were exempted from military service through all sorts of loopholes, an unfair reality laid bare before his eyes. Because his wrist had been crushed in a press during his factory years, stopping the growth plate of the outer bone and leaving his arm deformed, he was declared exempt from military service. The army doctor examining him clicked his tongue at the X-rays; he was exempted because his arm was so badly damaged, but the fact that he had been exempted for an impaired body brought him sadness and bitterness rather than joy.

At his university graduation, to which he invited the whole family, even his father attended for the first time, and the whole family smiled broadly for commemorative photos. But he could not simply smile. His classmate Lee Young-jin, who had opened his eyes by first showing him the video of the truth about May 18 in Gwangju, was not there. Young-jin had led student protests against the dictatorship, been arrested and locked in a cold prison cell, and had even been expelled from the university. Thinking of the heartbreak of his friend's parents, who must have been counting the days until Young-jin lifted their poor family up, he went straight back to his lodging house after the ceremony and, with trembling hands, carefully wrote a letter of comfort. Although he himself, using his exam preparation as an excuse, had not been able to throw himself fully into the struggle in the streets, he put into the letter his deep respect for, and sense of debt to, a righteous friend who had given his youth to fighting injustice and was serving time in prison for it.

In the spring of 1986, his father's stomach cancer returned and he was hospitalized, and the doctors said he had only a few months to live. Facing his father lying in pain in his hospital bed, he was struck by bitter regret for all the years he had done nothing but resent a father who had stood in the way of his learning. In fact, when he had failed the bar exam and fallen into despair, it was his father who had emptied his carefully hidden emergency savings into his hand and encouraged him to try again. His heart ached endlessly for his father's rough life, lived tenaciously with the single goal of providing a home where the family could sleep in peace, and he remembered his father's face as he beamed and boasted to the neighbors when his son entered law school. Desperate to give his father the joy of his success before his father closed his eyes, he threw himself into his studies with everything he had, and that year he passed the first and second rounds of the bar exam in succession and finally passed it outright.

He leaned close to the ear of his father, who was barely clinging to life amid the agony of failing breath as if waiting for news of his son, and whispered that he had passed. From the dry eyes of his father, who was slipping out of consciousness under narcotic painkillers, tears quietly ran down. Only after hearing that his son had passed did his father pass away, as if setting down a heavy burden at last, on the very same date as his own birthday. Only then did he realize that the father he had seen all his life as a great mountain pressing down on him had in fact been a mighty pillar shielding him from the storms of the world with his whole body, and the sorrow of letting him go without once saying a warm word of love while his father was alive remained a deep regret in his heart.
