After Daeyang Industrial closed, he joined Orient, one of the three largest watchmakers in the Seongnam industrial complex. There, as if by a miracle, he met again his close friend Shim Jeong-un, with whom he had attended the cram school for the high-school entrance equivalency exam. Realizing that even a high-school diploma would only take a factory worker so far toward becoming a manager, the two boy workers resolved to study hard together, get into university, and escape the factory life they so loathed. But opening a book inside the factory was not easy. Co-workers and the foreman, jealous, hurled abuse and smacked them on the back of the head, telling factory boys to know their place. To escape the harassment he moved his workstation to the sealed plating room, out of everyone's sight, and making the most of his quick hands, he finished his set quota in no time and used the time left over to keep studying. On the way home he and Jeong-un recited aloud lines by the poet Yun Dong-ju from their textbooks, and though their circumstances were at rock bottom, they held on to a firm hope that effort could change their lives.

Combining work with cram school was a path of thorns. Because of the factory's frequent unpaid overtime he kept missing classes and had to risk leaving work early. But the biggest obstacle was his father. When he read late at night, his father yelled at him to turn off the light because electricity was a waste of money, and when he had missed a few days of classes, his father thundered at him to go and get the fee reduced. Worn out by a situation in which, far from going to university, he could not even get help with cram school fees, he was about to quit after two months. Then Kim Chang-gu, the director of Seongil Academy, heard about his circumstances, told him that if he wanted to study he should simply keep coming without paying, and waived his fees entirely. This first warm recognition and unconditional encouragement from the world, offered to a poor factory boy with no connections at all, became a light of hope he would never forget, and it remained a great source of moral support in his life all the way until he passed the bar exam.

Then one day he heard that Shim Jeong-un, the friend he trusted and relied on most, had fallen in with a delinquent crowd at the factory and was drinking and smoking. Because Jeong-un was the only companion who had helped him endure that hellish reality, his sense of betrayal and sorrow was beyond words. He believed that no matter how harsh a factory boy's life was, no one had the right to wreck his own life, so he went to Jeong-un's rented room, told him gravely that their friendship was over, and stopped visiting the room that had been his only refuge, which he used to visit every day, putting silent pressure on him. It was a desperate shock treatment born of love, meant to bring a friend he truly cared for back to where he belonged. Fortunately Jeong-un pulled himself together and returned to his studies, and later the two boy workers achieved the dazzling result of both being admitted to Chung-Ang University.

To study even harder he moved to the lacquer room, which few people entered because of the strong smell. In that double-sealed room he finished his quota quickly and buried himself in books, but from constantly breathing toxic chemicals in an enclosed space with no ventilation at all, he developed chronic headaches and eventually lost more than half his sense of smell, unable to smell even the scent of fruit. To make matters worse, the wrist once crushed by the press began to cause serious problems. The growth plate of the outer bone, injured during the years when bones grow fast, had stopped, while only the inner bone kept growing, so his arm bent visibly out of shape, and every night he groaned in excruciating pain that felt like his bone was being shaved away. Fearing that his ruined body would cost him even the university entrance equivalency exam, he asked to quit, but the company refused to let a skilled worker go, and he finally managed to leave the factory only a month before the exam.

After passing the equivalency exam he wanted to go to a cram school for university entrance, but his father would not allow it, telling him to go to a night junior college. When he stayed home instead of looking for work, his father forced him up at three every morning to clear rubbish in the Sangdaewon market and pull a cart collecting empty bottles and scrap. They were suffocating days, when he banged his head against the wall and threw his shoes in fury, but with a nature that would not be cowed even by unfair pressure, he never gave up on going to a regular university. One dawn in a pouring rain, his mother found him shivering asleep in wet clothes on a stall under the market eaves and ran to him, bursting into tears; and beyond her, watching the lonely back of his father silently clearing rubbish in the rain, he buried in his heart a complicated, aching affection for a father who had thrown his whole body into providing for the family.

But the excruciating pain in his wrist, the fear of becoming disabled and unable to use his arm for the rest of his life, and his father's cold refusal to pay for treatment while he fixated only on buying a house, broke the seventeen-year-old boy completely. At the edge of a cliff of poverty and physical disability, unable to find any hope to go on living, he tried to end his life. After the first attempt failed, and under pressure from his father to go back to the Orient factory, he tried a second time and left a farewell note. But his brother-in-law, sensing something was wrong, came running and found him in time, and he later realized that he had survived because a pharmacist, sensing what the young boy intended, had given him digestive medicine instead of the sleeping pills he asked for. The heartfelt comfort of his poor brother-in-law and the quiet, nagging care of a stranger, the pharmacist, had pulled a fading life back from the brink.

Even as many of the boy workers around him went astray into drinking, smoking, violence and delinquency, what kept him from ever crossing the line was the overflowing love of a mother who believed in her son without conditions. He never skimmed even a little off his pay and handed his whole wage over to the family, and at fifteen he spent an entire month's pay on a strongman's so-called cure-all medicine to give to his mother, for which his father threw him out and he spent two nights under a wall outside. Later, when he gave up on university and was returning to the factory, he saved the fifty thousand won of pocket money he had left and slipped a thin gold ring onto his mother's finger; watching her touch it like a treasure through all her hardships, he learned deeply what it means for money to be spent in the most worthwhile way.

Around the time he had given up on university and was working at the factory, he was in despair because the Chun Doo-hwan military government's ban on private tutoring had cut off his way of earning tuition. Then Kim Chang-gu showed him a way forward: the new national entrance exam system strongly favored equivalency-exam students, and a scholarship scheme would soon follow. The expansion of university places and the special scholarship program announced soon after opened a miraculous path for the factory boy. He had just eight months left. He got permission to give only part of his wages to the family and spend the rest on cram school fees, and rushed to a university entrance cram school in Dapsimni. During that brutal stretch of sprinting until just before curfew and sleeping only three or four hours a night, he was beaten by a senior worker at the factory and suffered a broken rib. But with nowhere to retreat, he clutched his broken rib and stubbornly kept up both work and study. In the end he saved three months' wages, boldly quit the factory, and staked everything on studying full time.

In the reading room he fought mosquitoes in summer and the cold in winter, and to drive away sleep he waged a bloody battle, pressing thumbtacks into the desk at chest height until his study guides were stained with blood. To avoid drowsiness after eating, he cut back to just one of the warm lunch boxes his mother packed for him each day. His ranking, which at the start of his preparation had been below 300,000th nationwide, shot up to within the top 2,000 in eight months, and on an extraordinarily difficult national entrance exam he scored 285 points, among the very highest in the country. He proudly entered Chung-Ang University's College of Law as a special scholarship student, with his admission fee and tuition fully waived and a monthly living allowance of 200,000 won, three times his factory wage. Wanting to wear the school uniform he had never once been able to wear, he had a university uniform and cap made and wore them from two weeks before the entrance ceremony, and smiling broadly beside his mother in her finest hanbok, he stood at the starting line of a new life.
