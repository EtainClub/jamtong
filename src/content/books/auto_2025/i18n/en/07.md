Even facing threats to his family and the danger of having to carry a gas pistol in his pocket, he could not back down in the face of wrongdoing and corruption. It was a position as precarious as standing at the top of a hundred-foot pole, but it was clear that if a lawyer in the civic movement withdrew, no one would fight against those enormous forces, so he resolved to take one more step forward. To show that the essence of the Bundang Park View apartment preferential sale scandal was not a side issue but the rezoning corruption that had sent the value of the land soaring, he gave interviews to a broadcast news team. In the process he obtained a recording of a call in which the producer, posing as a prosecutor, spoke with the then mayor of Seongnam, and he made it public at a press conference. This exposure caused an uproar, and the wife of the provincial governor, senior officials, police officers and journalists were arrested one after another, but the prosecution instead charged him as an accomplice for helping the producer impersonate a prosecutor, and he was ultimately sentenced to a fine of 1.5 million won. It was the moment he touched a raw nerve of the real-estate power bloc, a great evil that colluded with lawbreaking to reap astronomical profits, and the author describes it as the starting point of an unending front against entrenched interests that continues to this day.

The fight against construction interests later continued into the Daejang-dong development project. In the author's account, it was the conservative party that had turned what was originally a public development by the Korea Land and Housing Corporation into a private one, and after he became mayor of Seongnam he blocked a private development that would have enriched private developers and turned it back into a public one. But when the city council's relentless opposition made it hard to use the city budget, he devised a public-private joint development model in which private developers bore all the risks and costs while Seongnam City secured a fixed profit first. According to the author's account, through this Seongnam City, having invested 2.5 billion won, ultimately recovered more than 550 billion won in development profits for its citizens, including 440 billion won, about seventy percent of the initially expected profit. He writes that this blocked a huge feast of unearned income for developers and speculators in politics and government, and that the speculators still bear a grudge over it and continue their all-out counterattacks. Because unearned income from real-estate speculation is someone's tears of blood, and a structural evil that leaves the honest, hardworking majority feeling deprived, he saw the fight to protect people's right to housing as a calling he could not abandon.

Even after the clash with the vast real-estate mafia, the cries of wronged citizens continued. When the two largest general hospitals in Seongnam's old downtown closed one after another, a grave crisis struck: the emergency medical facilities serving more than 500,000 residents disappeared almost overnight. Remembering the painful time in university when he could not afford a disability certificate and had to give up on treatment, he felt keenly that health care, on which people's lives depend, cannot be left to the market logic of profit alone, and he became co-chair of the committee to establish a Seongnam municipal hospital. Together with citizens, he threw himself into a citizens' initiative campaign in which, for the first time in the history of local self-government, residents would draft an ordinance themselves. In the bitter cold of midwinter he went out into the streets with a microphone to meet citizens, and in three weeks they achieved a remarkable feat, collecting more than 18,000 signatures for the citizens' initiative and drawing support signatures from well over 200,000 citizens.

At last, in March 2004, the ordinance to establish the Seongnam Medical Center was put before the plenary session of the city council. Because the ordinance carried the earnest hopes of hundreds of thousands of citizens, he sat in the observers' gallery full of hope, but the city council, in just forty-seven seconds and without even a debate for or against, rammed through a decision to defer deliberation, effectively scrapping the ordinance. Watching the blood and sweat of the citizens, the sovereign people, trampled in an instant, the citizens in the gallery rushed onto the floor of the chamber and poured out their anger. The council members fled in haste, and he sank down in the empty chamber and wept with the citizens. On top of that, the council filed criminal complaints against the citizens' representatives who had protested, accusing them of obstructing officials in the performance of their duties, and he had to take refuge in the basement of Jumin Church to work out the citizens' next steps.

In the cold church basement, when a hospital union official asked tearfully what they should do now, he found the answer that a movement that only shouts from outside cannot change the world. Just as you have to enter the tiger's den to catch the tiger, he concluded that citizens had to elect a mayor with their own strength and have that mayor build the hospital, and he made up his mind to enter real politics. This incident gave him a second criminal record, a fine, but he did not give up, and ten years later, in 2013, as mayor of Seongnam, he pressed the button for the groundbreaking blast of what he calls one of the finest public medical centers in the country, keeping his promise to the citizens. He describes this as proof in reality of his belief that public health care is not simply a deficit but the state's rightful duty to protect people's lives and health, and a good investment.

The price of standing up to entrenched interests to protect the public good was harsh. According to the author, under the Lee Myung-bak and Park Geun-hye governments he faced all kinds of raids, audits and prosecution investigations on roughly three out of every four days. He writes that they took not only his office and home but even his call records from overseas trips and the CCTV footage of his mother visiting city hall, and that it emerged that the Blue House and the Ministry of the Interior and Safety had drawn up a forty-page "Lee Jae-myung removal operation" report aimed at forcing him out of the mayor's office. He says that in his twelve years in public office he had to fight judicial persecution for all but two. Under surveillance like a goldfish in a bowl, where even the slightest wrongdoing would have finished him, he sees thorough integrity that left not a speck of blemish as the only shield that let him withstand the fierce persecution. He could have lived a comfortable life as an ordinary lawyer, but he quietly walked the path of standing with marginalized neighbors, and the great fight to build a world where ordinary people who work by the sweat of their brow are respected, he writes, is still going on today.
