After finishing the bar exam and the Judicial Research and Training Institute with excellent grades, he was qualified to be appointed a judge or prosecutor, but he gave this up without hesitation, convinced that once he had tasted even briefly the sweetness of jeongwan yeu, the favoritism shown to former judges and prosecutors after they enter private practice, he could never turn back. To keep his promise to his friends that he would become a human rights lawyer working for the weak, he decided to return to Seongnam, where he had spent his years as a worker, and open his practice there. But he had no money at all. Cho Young-rae, a lawyer whose class action for flood victims in Mangwon-dong he had helped with during his apprenticeship at the institute, admired the young man's courage and readily lent him five million won. On top of that, Kim Chang-gu, the director of Seongil Academy who had taught him without charging fees during his equivalency exam days, arranged another five million won, so he was able to raise ten million won. With the unconditional trust of these two elders as his capital, he opened an office in Seongnam and set up, next to his nameplate, a framed motto reading "Advocacy for the people's livelihood," firmly resolving to defend only people and justice, never money and power.

There was no way a green twenty-five-year-old lawyer with no connections would get ordinary cases at first. But labor cases erupting from the harsh working conditions of the Seongnam industrial complex, and cases of students arrested for activism, came flooding in. With no one else in the eastern Gyeonggi region willing to defend political prisoners and prisoners of conscience, he took on almost every human rights case, and since his clients could not even pay for photocopies, he covered the costs out of his own pocket and kept defending them for free. Calls requesting free consultations poured in through directory assistance, and when wronged citizens came to him, he kindly showed them ways to resolve matters themselves instead of through costly lawsuits. He spent more than half a year on the F-Coa Korea case, in which the company had siphoned off production and staged a sham closure to break the union, and won back the rights of the women factory workers. He also spent more than a year, digging even into the provisions of the International Labour Organization, on behalf of Ariel Galak, a Filipino worker who lost his right arm in an accident at a Seongnam factory and, because he was undocumented, nearly faced deportation without a single won for treatment. In the end, against the odds, he won recognition of the injury as an industrial accident and sent the compensation to a faraway country, and he drank a glass thinking of his own injured arm.

Alongside his legal practice, he threw himself without hesitation into supporting the labor movement in Yeoju and Icheon near Seongnam. At the request of An Yang-ro, who asked him to open a labor counseling center, he went down to Icheon twice a week to give free legal consultations and spend time with workers. When the Icheon labor counseling center was about to be evicted under pressure from the building owner, he readily put up a twenty-million-won deposit, even though he himself had borrowed money to open his practice, and for more than three years he quietly gave a million won every month toward the staff's living costs and the center's running costs. Even after he began working with the Seongnam Citizens' Association, he made the livelihoods of activists devoted to the public good his top priority, making sure they received their salaries and the four social insurances. He was putting into practice the value of sharing he had learned from Cho Young-rae, from Kim Chang-gu, and from his mother, who had found comfort in touching the gold ring he once gave her.

Once he took on a case, even a free one others avoided, he dug into it harder than anyone. He was the only lawyer in Seongnam who bought every volume of the latest case-law collections that salesmen were peddling to law offices around the country, staying up all night to study them and underline them. Having long carried a sense of guilt that he had not joined the student movement in university, he kept his promise seven years later by opening a labor counseling center attached to his law office and inviting his old friend Lee Young-jin to run it. Thanks to meticulous legal analysis and thorough study of precedents his success rate was very high, so high that company heads who had lost to him in court were impressed enough to refer civil cases to him. He searched wholeheartedly for solutions even in free consultations where he took no money, out of a desperate sense of responsibility that he had to be the last line of defense for poor neighbors who would have nowhere to go if he turned them away.

In 1991, buried in work and court cases, amid endless fatigue and pent-up anger, he decided to marry to open a new chapter in his life. Of five blind dates, he lost his heart at first sight to the third woman he met, Kim Hye-kyung. Completely taken with her gentle, cheerful and bright energy, he spent every evening with her and proposed on their fourth meeting. When she could not easily make up her mind, he handed her all six of his diaries, written over ten years from his factory days at fifteen until he passed the bar at twenty-four. He laid everything bare, his poor upbringing, his family's hard history, even wounds he might have wanted to hide, and asked for the trust to spend a lifetime together. Moved by his sincerity, she accepted, and they married seven months after they first met; meeting his wife became the best choice he ever made and the greatest refuge of his life.

From the mid-1990s he broadened his view to the civic movement and became chair of the executive committee of the Seongnam Citizens' Association. During this time he squarely confronted the Park View preferential sale scandal, in which land in Bundang's Baekgung-Jeongja district zoned for business use was illegally rezoned for housing so that construction interests and figures in politics and government could reap enormous profits from the apartment sales. As he dug into the truth, he uncovered a vast chain of corruption in which a huge construction mafia was entangled with powerful agencies, prosecutors and the press. According to the author, the construction interests tried to win him over by offering two billion won to set up a citizens' news outlet, and he turned them down with a mocking joke, telling those who wanted to buy a person's conscience with money that they would have to bring five hundred billion won. When bribery did not work, he writes, indiscriminate death threats began. Threatening calls came constantly to his office and his home, naming even the elementary school and the classes his young children attended. With even the police colluding with the construction interests so that he could not count on their protection, he obtained an official permit and carried a six-shot gas pistol in his suit pocket to protect his family and himself as he kept up a dangerous fight against enormous power.
