The author spent his childhood in Jitong village, Dochon-ri, Yean-myeon, deep in the mountains of Andong, North Gyeongsang Province. It was a remote village of slash-and-burn farmers where not even buses ran, and getting to Samgye Elementary School meant walking a rough 12-kilometer mountain path, there and back, every day. When the weather was bad or the bridge was under water he could not get to school, and sometimes he and his older brother used the weather as an excuse to turn back and run around the hills and fields instead. He caught freshwater shrimp in the stream and boiled unripe wild peaches to eat, growing up as innocently as a wild animal in the middle of nature. Poor as they were, the unspoiled landscape would later remain a great comfort in his life. But after his father lost even their small field to gambling, around the time he finished elementary school the whole family left their hometown to follow his father, who had already gone ahead to Seongnam.

Among his siblings he was the one who was especially affectionate and sweet with his mother. Whenever he came home he went to her first and threw himself into her arms, chattering about the books he had borrowed from the library, and he was showered with her love. His belief that poverty must never be allowed to shake a family's affection, and that love grows the more it is expressed, came from the deep love and support he received from his mother as a child.

But the family's circumstances grew worse by the day, and meals were often nothing more than coarse cakes made from barley bran. He could not even bring basic school supplies such as crayons or drawing paper, so during art classes and outdoor activities he had to sit alone in an empty classroom, enduring loneliness and a sense of being left out. During a Saemaul Movement beautification project, he was slapped across the face twenty-seven times by a teacher because he had not planted the flowers properly, having been busy helping at home; even at that young age he felt the injustice of it, and he held firm without bowing his head. The discrimination and unfairness he suffered for being poor taught him that one has to stand up to the world to protect what matters.

There is also the story of how his mother, so busy making a living that she was unsure of his birthday, went to a fortune-teller carrying a measure of unhulled barley. The fortune-teller offered the kind words that if she raised the child well, he would one day succeed greatly and she would live in comfort, and his mother held on to this vague hope deep in her heart like a religious faith. Together with the neighbors' remarks that his fine ears marked him as someone destined for great things, the firm belief his mother never stopped giving him became the root of a strong self-confidence that later let him keep trying, unbroken, through all kinds of seemingly impossible hardships.

In elementary school there were also teachers who showed him warm consideration. His fifth-grade homeroom teacher and the principal, learning that he was about to give up the school trip because of poverty, came to his home in person to persuade him. The teachers gave the children work such as clearing stones from the riverbed and cutting barley so that they could earn the trip money themselves, and they had the whole student body run the school store, using the profits to cover the costs of the poorer students. Thanks to this, the entire school went together on their first school trip, to Gyeongju, without a single child left out. This experience, in which everyone enjoyed the same opportunity regardless of family circumstances and felt a sense of achievement through their own effort and the cooperation of the community, later became the starting point for his values of welfare for all and cooperation.

In February 1976, right after finishing elementary school, he moved with his family to a rented room on a hillside in Sangdaewon-dong, Seongnam, where people evicted from the outskirts of Seoul had settled. For a thirteen-year-old boy who came up to the city carrying a can of bicycle-repair tools instead of a school bag, there was no path to further schooling. He immediately took a job at a tiny home-based necklace factory in a back alley of Sangdaewon-dong, where every day he sat around a coal-briquette stove soldering while breathing in toxic hydrochloric acid and vaporized lead. Moving to a place that promised higher pay, he worked twelve hours a day, from 8:30 in the morning until past 9 at night. When he came home exhausted, his mother, who had toiled all day cleaning a public toilet at the market and collecting the fees, would set the table for him. Watching his family's sacrifice and suffering, he endured each hard day, and the conviction that he had to build a strong community to protect the weak and the poor was etched into his bones.
